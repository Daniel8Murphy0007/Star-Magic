Here’s how astronomers determine a star’s mass:
•	Binary Star Systems (Kepler’s Laws): The most direct method is observing stars in binary systems, where two stars orbit each other. By applying Kepler’s Third Law, astronomers calculate mass using the orbital period and distance between the stars. The formula is:
M1+M2=4π2r3GT2
where 
M1+M2
is the total mass of the two stars, (r) is the separation distance, (T) is the orbital period, and (G) is the gravitational constant. By measuring the stars’ relative motions (e.g., via Doppler shifts in their spectra), individual masses can be derived if the system’s geometry is known.
•	Gravitational Effects on Nearby Objects: For isolated stars, mass can be inferred by observing the motion of objects in their gravitational field, like planets or companion stars. For example, the star’s mass affects the orbits of exoplanets, detectable through radial velocity or transit timing variations.
•	Stellar Models and Luminosity: For single stars, mass is estimated indirectly using stellar evolution models. A star’s luminosity, temperature, and spectral type correlate with its mass. Massive stars burn hotter and brighter, so by measuring their light output and surface temperature (via spectroscopy), astronomers use theoretical models to estimate mass. This is less precise but useful for distant stars.
•	Gravitational Lensing: In rare cases, a star’s mass can bend light from a background object (gravitational microlensing). The degree of bending depends on the star’s mass, allowing an estimate.
