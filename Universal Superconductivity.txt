Science’s Knowledge of Superconductivity

Fundamentals
Superconductivity is a quantum phenomenon where certain materials, when cooled below a critical temperature (\(T_c\)), exhibit zero electrical resistance and expel magnetic fields (the Meissner effect). Discovered by Heike Kamerlingh Onnes in 1911 with mercury (\(T_c = 4.2 \, \text{K}\)), it’s now understood as a macroscopic quantum state involving paired electrons.

	Cooper Pairs: In conventional superconductors (e.g., lead, niobium), electrons, which normally repel each other, form pairs via lattice vibrations (phonons). This pairing, explained by BCS theory (Bardeen, Cooper, Schrieffer, 1957), allows electrons to move without scattering, zeroing resistance. The binding energy of these pairs defines \(T_c\), typically below 30 K for conventional materials.
	Meissner Effect:** Superconductors become perfect diamagnets below \(T_c\), expelling magnetic fields. This levitates magnets (e.g., in maglev trains) and is tied to the London equations, describing how supercurrents screen external fields.

Types of Superconductors
	Type I: Simple metals (e.g., mercury, lead) with low \(T_c\) (1–10 K) and a sharp transition to superconductivity. They expel fields completely but break down under modest magnetic fields (critical field \(H_c \approx 0.1 \, \text{T}\)).
	Type II: Alloys and compounds (e.g., niobium-titanium, \(T_c = 9.2 \, \text{K}\); YBCO, \(T_c = 92 \, \text{K}\)) with higher \(T_c\) and two critical fields (\(H_{c1}\), \(H_{c2}\)). Between these, magnetic flux penetrates as quantized vortices, allowing practical applications like MRI magnets (up to 20 T).

High-Temperature Superconductors (HTS)
Discovered in 1986 by Bednorz and Müller with LaBaCuO (\(T_c = 35 \, \text{K}\)), HTS materials like YBa₂Cu₃O₇ (YBCO, \(T_c = 92 \, \text{K}\)) operate above liquid nitrogen’s boiling point (77 K). These cuprates:
	Structure: Layered perovskites with copper-oxygen planes where superconductivity occurs.
	Mechanism: Unlike BCS, pairing isn’t purely phonon-mediated; d-wave symmetry suggests a complex, possibly magnetic or electronic origin. The pseudogap phase (above \(T_c\)) hints at pre-paired states, but the exact glue remains debated.
	Applications: Power cables (e.g., Nexans’ 2023 HTS grid projects), fusion magnets (e.g., MIT’s SPARC, aiming for 20 T at 20 K by 2025), and levitating trains.

Exotic Superconductors

	Hydrides: Under extreme pressure (e.g., 155 GPa), H₃S reaches \(T_c = 203 \, \text{K}\) (2015), and LaH₁₀ hits 260 K (-13°C, 2020). These confirm phonon-mediated pairing but require impractical conditions (1–2 million atm).

Experimental Milestones

	Tunneling Spectroscopy: STM (scanning tunneling microscopy) on Bi₂Sr₂CaCu₂O₈ (BSCCO) maps Cooper pair wavefunctions, showing d-wave gaps (Millis, 2023).
	Neutron Scattering: Probes lattice vibrations in Nb (2022 ILL data) and magnetic fluctuations in cuprates, linking pairing to material structure.
