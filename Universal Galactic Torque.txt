Universal Galactic Torque

Calculating the torque exerted by our Solar System on the Milky Way galaxy is an intriguing but complex problem, as torque depends on the force applied, the distance from the pivot point (lever arm), and the system's dynamics. Since the Solar System orbits the galactic center, we can interpret "torque" here as the gravitational torque associated with its orbital motion around the Milky Way’s center, specifically the super-massive black hole Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*) and the galaxy’s mass distribution. However, in a stable circular orbit, the net torque is typically zero because the gravitational force acts radially toward the center, perpendicular to the angular momentum. Let’s explore this step-by-step, define an equation, and provide a solution based on reasonable assumptions as of March 18, 2025.
Conceptual Framework
	Torque Definition: Torque (τ) is the rotational equivalent of force, given by τ=r×F, where r is the position vector from the pivot point, F is the force, and × denotes the cross product. In magnitude, τ=rFsin⁡θ, where θ is the angle between r and F.
	Solar System’s Orbit: The Solar System orbits the galactic center at about 26,000 light-years (8 kiloparsecs) with a speed of approximately 230 km/s, taking roughly 225 million years per orbit. The primary force is gravity from the galaxy’s mass inside this radius, dominated by stars, gas, and dark matter—not just Sgr A*.
	Pivot Point: We’ll assume the galactic center (Sgr A*) as the pivot, though the distributed mass complicates this.
	Steady-State Orbit: In a circular orbit, the gravitational force provides centripetal acceleration, and θ=90° for the cross product, but the net torque is zero because the system is in equilibrium. Torque would arise if the orbit were perturbed (e.g., by a passing galaxy or misalignment), but we’ll calculate a hypothetical torque based on gravitational force and test the equilibrium assumption.
Step 1: Define the Equation

The gravitational force between the Solar System and the Milky Way’s mass interior to its orbit is: 
F=G〖(M〗_gal M_sol/r^2)

	G=6.67430×〖10〗^(-11) m^3 〖kg〗^(-1) s^(-2 )(gravitational constant),
	M_sol= mass of the Milky Way interior to the Solar System’s orbit,
	M_sol=1.989×〖10〗^30 kg (Solar System mass, approximated as the Sun’s mass since it dominates),
	r=8 kpc=2.468×〖10〗^20 m (distance to galactic center, converting 8 kpc using 1 pc = 3.0857×〖10〗^16 m.

The mass interior to the Solar System’s orbit is estimated from its orbital dynamics. Using the orbital speed v=230 km/s=2.3×〖10〗^5 m/s and assuming a circular orbit:

Cancel M_sol and solve for M_gal : 
v^2/r=G〖(M〗_gal/r^2)
 
M_gal=(v^2 r)/G

Step 3: Calculate Torque

Now, compute the torque: 
τ=G〖(M〗_gal M_sol/r) 

\tau = 6.67430 \times 10^{-11} \cdot \frac{(1.99 \times 10^{41}) \cdot (1.989 \times 10^{30})}{2.468 \times 10^{20}}}

Step 4: Interpret the Result

	Units: Newton-meters (N•m), the standard unit of torque.
	Magnitude: 1.07×〖10〗^41 N\cdotpm is enormous, reflecting the vast scales involved.
	Reality Check: In a stable circular orbit, the net torque should be zero because the gravitational force is radial, and angular momentum is conserved. This calculated torque represents the instantaneous gravitational torque if we consider the force as causing rotational acceleration, but since the orbit isn’t accelerating (i.e., no change in angular velocity), the actual net torque is zero. The value above is a theoretical maximum if the system were not in equilibrium.

Final Equation and Solution

Equation:

Actual Net Torque: In the Solar System’s stable orbit, τ_net=0 N\cdotpm, as the gravitational force balances the centripetal requirement.

Caveats

	Distributed Mass: The Milky Way’s mass isn’t all at Sgr A* (4.1 million solar masses); it’s spread across a dark matter halo, stars, and gas, complicating the point-mass assumption.
	Non-Circular Effects: The orbit isn’t perfectly circular, and perturbations (e.g., spiral arms) could introduce small torques, but these are minor compared to the galactic scale.
