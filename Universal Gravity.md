How we currently measure Newtonian Gravity

Gravity in celestial bodies is measured primarily through its effects on other objects and the application of Newton’s law of universal gravitation, which states that the gravitational force (F) between two masses (m₁ and m₂) is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance (r) between their centers: F = G * (m₁ * m₂ / r²), where G is the gravitational constant (approximately 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻²).
Here’s how we measure it in practice:
	Orbital Motion: For planets, moons, or stars, we observe how objects (like satellites or other celestial bodies) move in their gravitational fields. The speed and shape of an orbit depend on the mass of the central body. For example, by studying how fast a moon orbits a planet and the distance between them, we can calculate the planet’s mass using Kepler’s Third Law (refined by Newton): the square of the orbital period (T²) is proportional to the cube of the orbital radius (r³), and the constant of proportionality involves the central body’s mass. Rearranging these equations lets us solve for mass, and thus gravity.
	Surface Gravity: On a celestial body’s surface, gravity is measured as the acceleration experienced by an object in free fall, typically in meters per second squared (m/s²). For Earth, this is about 9.8 m/s². For other bodies, we infer this from their mass (M) and radius (R) using the formula g = G * M / R². Mass is often derived from orbital data, while radius can be measured through direct observation (e.g., telescopes or spacecraft imaging).
	Spacecraft Measurements: Space missions provide precise data. Flybys or orbiting probes detect tiny changes in velocity caused by a body’s gravity, a technique called gravitational perturbation analysis. For example, NASA’s GRAIL mission mapped the Moon’s gravity by tracking the distance between two orbiting spacecraft as they responded to mass variations below. Similarly, accelerometers or gravimeters on landers (like those used on the Moon or Mars) directly measure local gravitational acceleration.
	Mass Distribution: Larger bodies aren’t perfectly uniform, so gravity varies across their surfaces. We study these anomalies (e.g., Earth’s geoid or the Moon’s mascons) using satellite data or surface measurements to understand internal structure, which refines our overall gravity estimates.
	Exotic Cases: For objects like black holes, gravity is inferred indirectly via its extreme effects—such as the bending of light (gravitational lensing) or the orbital behavior of nearby stars. The Event Horizon Telescope’s imaging of black hole shadows relies on these principles.
In short, we measure gravity by combining observed motions, physical laws, and direct or remote sensing, tailoring the method to the body in question—whether it’s a planet, star, or something stranger. Each approach builds on the same core idea: gravity reveals itself through its pull.
